What companion planting does for your vegetable bed

In companion planting, two or more vegetables grow in the same bed, in neighbouring rows or alternating. The opposite is a monoculture, where a whole bed holds only carrots or only cabbage.

The Austrian leaflet «Mischkultur» from Natur im Garten lists several benefits. The scent of some plants is said to confuse pests. Flowering neighbours attract beneficial insects such as hoverflies, lacewings and ladybirds. Tall plants shade the soil, so less water evaporates and weeds struggle. And fast crops such as radishes or lettuce are harvested before the main crop needs the space.

One benefit is undisputed: the space is used better and the soil stays covered. The Bavarian Garden Academy, which is otherwise rather cautious, says so too.

Companion planting chart: good and bad neighbours

For the chart we compared two leaflets: «Mischkultur» from Natur im Garten (Lower Austria, 2023) and the planting plan «Gute und schlechte Nachbarn» from BUND (Germany, 2026). A pair is marked only if both say the same. An empty square does not mean «bad»; it means the two sources say nothing or say different things.

Where the leaflets contradict each other

Put several companion planting charts side by side and you soon find contradictions. The Bavarian Garden Academy writes in its leaflet that reports from experience often contradict each other. These pairs are listed differently by Natur im Garten and BUND:

PairNatur im GartenBUND
Radishes and cabbageunfavourablegood
Lettuce and celeryunfavourablegood (head lettuce)
Carrots and celery or parsleyunfavourablenot listed as bad
Tomatoes and cucumbersunfavourablenot listed
Leeks and onionsunfavourablenot listed

Even the best-known pair is disputed. Onions and carrots are said to protect each other from onion fly and carrot fly. Both leaflets recommend them as neighbours. The Bavarian Garden Academy advises against it: the two need different amounts of water and nutrients, and the protection against the flies works only in a few cases.

Our advice: treat the companion planting chart as a collection of ideas, not as a law. According to the Bavarian Garden Academy, site, soil and care probably matter more than the neighbour.

Five rules for a good mixed bed

A few basic rules matter more than individual pairs. They come from the leaflets of BUND and the Bavarian Garden Academy:

  1. Mix the appetites. Put heavy feeders next to light feeders, for example cabbage next to lettuce. That way neighbours compete less for nutrients. Which vegetables belong to which group is explained in the crop rotation guide.
  2. Mix the roots. Deep-rooted crops such as carrots go well next to shallow-rooted ones such as lettuce or radishes.
  3. Separate the families. Closely related vegetables share pests and diseases. Cabbage next to kohlrabi therefore does little good.
  4. Use the time. Fast crops such as radishes, spinach or lettuce must be harvested before the main crop crowds them.
  5. Leave space. According to the Bavarian Garden Academy, the most common mistake is sowing too close. Plan with the plants' final size and leave about 15 centimetres for hoeing.

Example: a mixed bed with peas, carrots and sweetcorn

The Bavarian Garden Academy describes three simple combinations you can try straight away:

  • Peas and radishes: one row of radishes between two rows of peas.
  • Carrots and radishes: radishes between the carrots, with about 15 centimetres between the drills. The radishes are harvested after a few weeks and make room.
  • Sweetcorn and spinach: sow spinach in early March under fleece and harvest it by mid-May. The sweetcorn goes in between in early May and stands until mid-September.

It works the same way in a raised bed. Because space is tight there, planning with early and late crops is especially worthwhile.

Frequently asked questions

What should not be planted next to tomatoes?

Natur im Garten advises against cucumbers next to tomatoes; BUND does not list this pair. Potatoes are related to tomatoes and share diseases, so they are better placed in a different bed.

What can I plant next to carrots?

Natur im Garten and BUND list onions, peas and leeks as good neighbours. Radishes work as a fast catch crop. The Bavarian Garden Academy advises against onions next to carrots because they need different amounts of water.

Is companion planting scientifically proven?

Only partly. It is proven that space is used better and the soil stays covered. Whether vegetables help each other is hard to demonstrate, according to the Bavarian Garden Academy. Most charts are based on experience.

Does the companion planting chart apply to herbs too?

Partly. Both leaflets list dill as a good neighbour for onions. According to NABU, dill and basil do not get on. For many herbs there is no clear information.
