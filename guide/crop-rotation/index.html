Crop rotation: a simple four-year plan for your vegetable beds

By Silvan, Beetario · updated 24 November 2026 · 6 min read

Crop rotation means that the same plant family does not grow in the same bed every year. You move the groups round, usually over four years, from heavy feeders to medium and light feeders and then a green manure. That way the soil is not drained of the same nutrients, and diseases such as clubroot do not find their host plant every year.

Why crop rotation matters in the vegetable garden

Every plant family takes different nutrients from the soil in different amounts. If the same crop grows in the same place year after year, the soil is depleted unevenly. Gardeners call this soil sickness: plants grow more weakly even though you feed them.

Soil-borne pests and diseases matter even more. Many attack only one plant family and stay in the bed for several years. The Royal Horticultural Society gives clubroot in brassicas and white rot in onions as examples. If the host plant is missing for a few years, these problems decline.

Rotation does not replace good care, but it costs nothing except a little planning. It also makes planning easier: if you know what grew where last year, you do not have to guess in spring.

Crop rotation by nutrient need: heavy, medium and light feeders

The simplest rotation follows how hungry the plants are. Divide your vegetable area into four beds of similar size. Each year every group moves on by one bed, and after four years the cycle starts again.

The grouping below follows the Austrian leaflet «Fruchtfolge im Gemüsegarten» from Natur im Garten. It also says how much mature compost suits each group per square metre and year: 4 to 6 litres for heavy feeders, 2 to 4 litres for medium feeders and no more than 1.5 litres for light feeders.

The boundaries are not drawn the same way everywhere. Natur im Garten counts onions as medium feeders, NABU as light feeders, and the Royal Horticultural Society treats them as a group of their own. For your plan this does not matter: what counts is that the same family does not return to the same place the following year.

Plant families: which vegetables are related

For diseases, what counts is not nutrient need but kinship. Kohlrabi after broccoli is therefore no rotation, even if the names sound different: both are brassicas and both are susceptible to clubroot. NABU advises against growing brassicas in the same bed two years running.

The family matters for green manure too. Yellow mustard is a brassica and so is no good as green manure after cabbage. Phacelia, buckwheat or clover are better choices there.

How to set up your crop rotation plan

  1. Count your beds. Divide the vegetable area into three or four beds of similar size. A raised bed counts as a bed of its own.
  2. Leave out perennials. Rhubarb and asparagus stay in the same place for many years and are not part of the rotation.
  3. Allocate the groups. In year one, give each bed one group: heavy feeders, medium feeders, light feeders and green manure.
  4. Move on every year. Each group moves one bed further, always in the same direction.
  5. Write it down. Note every year what grew where. Without notes, nobody remembers in year three where the potatoes were.

The Royal Horticultural Society works with families rather than nutrient groups and gives a simple rule: brassicas follow legumes, onions and roots; these follow potatoes; potatoes follow brassicas. With only three beds this works as a three-year plan. Both approaches work, as long as you change the families.

What can move freely and what cannot

Not every vegetable has to follow the rotation strictly. According to the Royal Horticultural Society, cucurbits, salads and sweetcorn can go anywhere, as long as they are not grown in the same place too often. Peppers and aubergines can also go anywhere, even though they are nightshades.

Small gardens rarely have four equal beds. A simple rule then helps: keep the same family out of the same place for at least three years. Fast crops such as radishes or lettuce fill the gaps. Which neighbours get on well in one bed is covered in the companion planting chart.

For larger gardens, the Bavarian Garden Academy suggests a bed of summer flowers that moves to a new place every year. It loosens up the rotation and brings flowers for insects.

Frequently asked questions

How many years does a crop rotation take?

Usually three or four years. With four beds, heavy feeders, medium feeders, light feeders and green manure take turns. With three beds, follow the Royal Horticultural Society rule: brassicas, then legumes, onions and roots, then potatoes.

Do I need crop rotation in a raised bed?

Yes, if you use it for several years. Pests and diseases stay in the soil of a raised bed too. Many gardeners start with heavy feeders in year one and then move on to medium and light feeders.

What should I plant after tomatoes?

The next year, a medium feeder suits that spot, for example carrots, beetroot, fennel or leeks. Potatoes, peppers and aubergines are related to tomatoes and should not follow them.

Are herbs part of the rotation?

Annual herbs such as basil, dill or parsley move with the rotation. Dill and parsley are umbellifers like the carrot. Perennial herbs such as chives or thyme stay where they are.
