Raised bed planner: what to plant and when

By Silvan, Beetario · updated 24 November 2026 · 5 min read

A raised bed planner starts with the age of the filling. In the first year the fresh compost releases a lot of nitrogen. That suits hungry vegetables such as tomatoes, peppers, cabbage, leeks and celery. From the second year you feed lightly and switch to lettuce, herbs and root vegetables.

What is different about a raised bed

A raised bed is more than a bed lifted off the ground. The sun warms its side walls, so the soil warms up earlier in spring. According to the Bavarian State Institute for Viticulture and Horticulture (LWG), you can therefore start early, and slugs largely stay away.

The downside: the soil dries out faster than in a bed in the ground. The Royal Horticultural Society points this out. In summer you therefore have to water more often.

Size matters for planting too. The LWG recommends a width of no more than 1 to 1.2 metres, so that you can reach the middle from both sides. The RHS keeps raised beds under 1.5 metres wide for the same reason. Around 80 centimetres is a comfortable working height.

Raised bed planner for the first year

The classic filling consists of brushwood, compost and good garden soil. In the first year a lot of nitrogen is released. The LWG therefore recommends heavy feeders, vegetables with a high nutrient need, to begin with.

According to the LWG, 100 to 200 grams of hoof and horn is one or two heaped handfuls per square metre. If you mulch with green cuttings, you need less fertiliser. Which vegetables belong to which group and how to continue after year two is covered in the crop rotation guide.

Planting calendar: the raised bed from March to autumn

Because the soil between the walls warms up early, the season starts as early as late March. The LWG divides the year into three parts:

  • Spring, from late March: sow radishes, carrots, mooli and rocket. Plant cut-and-come-again lettuces such as oakleaf or lollo and cover them with fleece on frosty nights.
  • Early summer, April to June: sow chard and dwarf beans, plant beetroot, broccoli and lettuce. Warmth-loving vegetables such as bush tomatoes, courgettes, squash, peppers and chillies only from mid-May.
  • High summer, June to August: sow radishes, spinach and mooli. Plant kohlrabi, endive, Chinese cabbage and lamb's lettuce.

Whether mid-May is really safe depends on where you live. How to judge it is explained in the guide to the last frost date.

Raised bed planner: dividing up the space

Space is tight on one or two square metres. The LWG gives some practical tips:

  • South side: annual, warmth-loving herbs such as basil.
  • Short ends: perennial crops such as chives or alpine strawberries that you do not replant every year.
  • Spacing: tomatoes, courgettes and peppers need 50 to 60 centimetres.
  • Varieties: cut-and-come-again lettuces do better than hearting lettuce. For cucumbers and peppers, small-fruited snack varieties suit. Without a rain cover, choose tomatoes tolerant of late blight.
  • Do not crowd: young plants that are overgrown by their neighbours stay stunted, according to the LWG. Thin carrots and other sowings early.

Which vegetables make good neighbours is shown in the companion planting chart. Fast crops such as radishes between slow ones such as carrots use the space twice.

Watering and care in summer

When the bed is densely planted in summer, the leaves give off a lot of water and the soil needs constant topping up. As a rule of thumb in hot, settled weather, the LWG gives 3 litres per square metre per day. For a bed of 1 by 2 metres that is about 6 litres a day.

Before watering, check with your finger whether the soil is dry a few centimetres down. A mulch of green cuttings keeps moisture in for longer. Tomatoes in particular want to be watered evenly, or the fruit splits.

In autumn the soil does not stay empty. What you planted in high summer, such as kohlrabi, endive, Chinese cabbage and lamb's lettuce, you harvest well into autumn. You can cover harvested patches with green cuttings. According to the LWG, such a mulch saves fertiliser.

According to the LWG, kale belongs in every bed of this kind: it stands into winter and is only harvested after the first frost.

Frequently asked questions

What should I plant in a raised bed in the first year?

Heavy feeders such as tomatoes, peppers, cabbage, leeks and celery. The fresh filling releases a lot of nitrogen in the first year, which these vegetables use well.

When can I start planting a raised bed?

From late March with radishes, carrots, rocket and cut-and-come-again lettuce, under fleece on frosty nights. Warmth-loving vegetables such as tomatoes, courgettes and peppers only go in from mid-May.

How often do I need to water a raised bed?

More often than a bed in the ground, because a raised bed dries out faster. In hot weather the LWG gives about 3 litres per square metre per day. Check with your finger first.

How wide should a raised bed be?

No more than 1 to 1.2 metres, so that you can reach the middle from both sides. The LWG gives a working height of about 80 centimetres.
